Reference deployments

**1. El Servidor Clásico de Control**

* **Función:** Ejecuta el sistema operativo y el software de control cuántico.
* **Físico:** Servidores comerciales de alto rendimiento (CPUs/GPUs) ubicados en el mismo centro de datos que la QPU para reducir la latencia de red.

**2. Electrónica de Control (El Traductor)**

* **Función:** Transforma los unos y ceros digitales en ondas físicas que los qubits puedan entender.
* **Físico:** Chasis que contiene **FPGAs** (Field Programmable Gate Arrays), **DACs** (Convertidores Digital-Analógico de velocidad ultraalta) y **ADCs** (Convertidores Analógico-Digital).
* **Operación:** Si el código clásico dice "aplica una compuerta de rotación en el Qubit 1", la FPGA calcula la forma de onda exacta y el DAC la genera como un pulso físico.

**3. Líneas de Transmisión (El Medio)**

* **Función:** Transportan las señales físicas hacia el interior del criostato sin introducir calor ni ruido.
* **Físico:** Cables coaxiales de alta fidelidad, atenuadores criogénicos y guías de onda que bajan por las distintas etapas de temperatura del refrigerador.

**4. El Procesador Cuántico (QPU)**

* **Función:** Recibe los pulsos, altera el estado de los qubits y permite la medición.
* **Físico:** Un chip de silicio o zafiro con circuitos superconductores (en tecnologías como IBM o Google) o trampas de átomos/iones (como IonQ).



SO i can build a thermodinamic computer on a fpga? or how ?  Yes or not?

Short answer: No—not in the sense of building a true thermodynamic computer like Extropic's TSU on an FPGA.

However, yes, you can build a digital emulator/prototype of a thermodynamic computer on an FPGA.

Here's the distinction:

Can you do this on an FPGA?	Yes/No
Simulate Gibbs sampling	✅ Yes
Implement Energy-Based Models (EBMs)	✅ Yes
Build pbits digitally using PRNGs	✅ Yes
Prototype the architecture of a TSU	✅ Yes
Build a real transistor-noise thermodynamic computer	❌ No
Get the same energy efficiency as Extropic	❌ No
Why not?

An FPGA is deterministic digital logic.

Extropic's hardware is fundamentally different.

Their TSUs rely on physical analog noise inside transistors. A pbit is not just a random number generator—it is a circuit whose output naturally fluctuates because of transistor physics.

That physical randomness is what makes it extremely energy efficient.

An FPGA cannot reproduce that because every LUT and flip-flop is deterministic.

What can you build?

Actually, quite a lot.

For example:

+----------------------------+
| FPGA                       |
|                            |
|  PRNG                      |
|      ↓                     |
|  Digital pbit              |
|      ↓                     |
| Gibbs Update Engine        |
|      ↓                     |
| Local Memory               |
|      ↓                     |
| Neighbor Communication     |
|      ↓                     |
| Repeat                     |
+----------------------------+

This behaves algorithmically like a TSU.

Instead of using transistor noise, you use:

LFSRs
Xorshift
ChaCha PRNG
Hardware RNG (if available)

to generate the random bits.

What would be missing?

Extropic's chip contains probabilistic circuits that directly implement probability distributions such as Bernoulli, Gaussian, categorical, and Gaussian mixtures in hardware rather than computing them digitally.

On an FPGA you would instead do

random_number
      ↓
compare with probability
      ↓
output 0 or 1

instead of

analog transistor
      ↓
naturally fluctuates
      ↓
output 0 or 1

The mathematical result is similar, but the implementation and energy consumption are very different.

Could you build an "open-source TSU" on an FPGA?

Yes.

In fact, this would be an excellent research project.

You could implement:

Thousands of digital pbits
Graph-colored block Gibbs sampling
Energy-Based Models
Boltzmann Machines
Ising models
Denoising Thermodynamic Models (DTMs)
A compiler that maps a graph directly to FPGA routing

The architecture would resemble what Extropic describes: many sampling cells communicating locally and repeatedly performing Gibbs updates.

Could it later become an ASIC?

Yes, and this is the typical hardware development path:

Algorithm
      ↓
Python (JAX)
      ↓
FPGA prototype
      ↓
RTL verification
      ↓
ASIC
      ↓
Thermodynamic ASIC

The FPGA is a way to validate the architecture and algorithms. Once they work, you could replace the digital pbits with true analog probabilistic circuits in a custom chip.

Since you have a Tang Nano 9K

Your Tang Nano 9K is powerful enough to prototype a small thermodynamic computing system, for example:

256–2048 digital pbits
Parallel Gibbs sampling engine
Small Ising models
Hopfield networks
Boltzmann machines
Energy-based optimization demos

It won't match Extropic's hardware in energy efficiency, but it can let you explore the same computational principles and develop the algorithms before moving to a custom ASIC.

This is actually the key idea behind thermodynamic computing.

A normal computer tries to eliminate noise.

A thermodynamic computer tries to use noise as the computation itself.

Every electronic circuit has noise.

Normal computers hate noise

They use

The entire semiconductor industry is built around fighting noise.

But why is this faster?

Suppose an AI model needs

10 trillion random samples

A GPU has to compute every one.

A thermodynamic computer gets those samples from physical noise that is already present in the device, so much less digital work is required for that part of the computation. This is one of the reasons Extropic argues their architecture can be dramatically more energy efficient for sampling-heavy workloads.

Why don't CPUs already do this?

Because CPUs are designed for exact arithmetic.

If you ask

2 + 2

you always expect

4

never

4

5

Randomness would break almost every conventional program.

However, AI and statistical algorithms often want randomness:

Gibbs sampling
Monte Carlo methods
Diffusion models
Boltzmann machines
Bayesian inference
Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)

These algorithms are fundamentally based on drawing samples from probability distributions rather than producing one exact deterministic answer.

This is why many researchers think thermodynamic computing could become a new kind of accelerator, much like GPUs became accelerators for matrix multiplication. CPUs remain best for deterministic computation, while thermodynamic hardware may excel at probabilistic computation where high-quality sampling is the main workload.
